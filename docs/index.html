Two Emojis of Difference

What Multilingual Affective Generation Benchmarks Actually Measure

Fardeen Sadab* Adib Sakhawat*
Department of Computer Science and Engineering, Islamic University of Technology, Dhaka, Bangladesh
* Equal contribution
Paper PDF arXiv 2609.29445 Code BibTeX

Abstract

We audit a multilingual affective generation benchmark—eight instruction-tuned LLMs producing emoji summaries for 17,100 Bangla, English and Hindi sentences, with 6,960 human judgements—and find its headline conclusions to be artefacts of the measurement instrument rather than properties of the systems. Treating annotators as a random rather than a fixed factor, no system differs significantly from any other (F(7, 14) = 0.59, p = 0.76), although the conventional analysis declares 19 of 28 pairwise differences significant. Annotator identity explains far more rating variance than system identity, and the winning system changes whenever any single annotator is removed. The ordering that does emerge tracks output length: mean emoji count explains 78.7% of between-system variance, and a within-item length-matched comparison over 2,599 pairs reverses the leaderboard. We further show that cross-provider anisotropy differences vanish under mean-centring, that per-language token costs change sign with the normalising unit, and that multi-view row-wise splits inflate macro-F1 by 3.1 points and change the top-ranked system. In place of preference scoring we propose emoji–affect decodability, a reference-based probe whose rankings are stable to ±0.003 macro-F1 across seeds.

Finding 1

Preference scores do not identify a winner

The choice of error term decides the result. Tested against the residual, the near-universal practice in NLP, the system effect looks overwhelming. Tested against the system×rater interaction, the correct term when annotators are a sample from a population, it vanishes. Annotators disagree about systems more than systems differ.

Conventional analysis

p < 10−16; 19 of 28 pairwise differences significant
Annotators as a random factor
F(7, 14) = 0.59
p = 0.76; zero pairwise differences significant

Who rates matters more than what is rated

On the common sum-of-squares scale, the annotator main effect accounts for 22.1% of the total and the system for 0.7%. Bias-sensitive agreement is near zero: Krippendorff’s αordinal = 0.014, ICC(2,1) = 0.116, despite Pearson correlations of 0.17–0.18.

Finding 2

The ratings track output length

Mean emoji count predicts MOS with ρ = 0.90, explaining 78.7% of between-system variance; each extra emoji is worth 0.159 MOS points. The full spread between best and worst system is 0.341 points, slightly more than two emojis’ worth of length. Re-sort the leaderboard to compare.

Proposal

Emoji–affect decodability

EAD(g, L) is the 5-fold cross-validated macro-F1 of an ℓ2-regularised multinomial logistic regression over sublinear TF-IDF features of the emojis generator g emits, predicting the corpus emotion label. It measures how much of the corpus’s affective distinction survives compression into the emoji channel, using labels the corpora already carry and no annotators.

A generator that emits the same three emojis for everything scores at the floor regardless of how pleasant those emojis are.

Stable
Largest per-system SD across ten seeds is 0.0033 macro-F1; bootstrap CIs separate systems into three tiers.
Language-specific
Transferring a decoder across languages costs 0.252 macro-F1 despite >99.7% vocabulary overlap. Models emit the same emojis across languages but use them to mean different things.
Competitive with embeddings
At matched sample size in Bangla, the best emoji channel reaches 0.673 macro-F1 against 0.595 for the best sentence embedding.
Finding 3

Three more claims that do not survive

Geometry
Anisotropy gaps are a mean offset
Raw anisotropy ranges from 0.149 to 0.700 across providers. After subtracting the global mean, every model lies in [−0.0008, 0.0013]. Language separability on non-parallel corpora is not cross-lingual alignment.
Cost
Token premia change sign with the unit
Per source byte, Bangla is the cheapest language (1.108 tokens). Per character, it costs 2.0× English, because Bangla averages 2.64 UTF-8 bytes per character.
Leakage
Row-wise splits inflate macro-F1 by 3.1 points
A naive 80/20 split gives 0.630 against 0.598 for a split grouped by source item, and the top-ranked system changes from Mistral-Large to Gemma-3-27B.
